# The Cyanotype of Us

> Draft · 3/3 scenes

The room was a monochrome scream, the blue light of Evelyn's vision cutting through the dark. Outside, the sky was a vast, impossible expanse of azure, and inside, only the sharp, hard outline of his silhouette existed. He tried to tell a story, his voice a warm, rustling thing, but to her ears it sounded like the static crackle of a photograph developing too fast.

"It's just funny, Evelyn," he said, leaning in, his breath ghosting against her shoulder. "Remember that time we got lost in the park?"

To Evelyn, it was nothing. Just a faint, flickering shadow against the blue curtain of the night. The colors of his laughter, the golden warmth of the memory, were all washed away in the stark contrast of her perception. She needed to fix it. She reached out, her hand hovering inches from his, not to touch skin, but to adjust the angle of the 'image' in their shared mind. She could feel him tense, his laughter dying down as the blue silence pressed in.

She needed to make the picture pop. She needed to make it real.

He stopped talking, his eyes searching hers in that impossible blue light. The air between them felt thin, waiting for something to break the spell. Evelyn's hand trembled slightly, the air above it shimmering like a developing emulsion. She was ready to reach out and fix the world, but then she heard the faint, distant smell of lavender from the kitchen.

Her hand froze. The static crackle of his voice seemed to hold just a little longer. The blue silence wasn't the whole truth. But if she let go of the angle, if she let him speak without her filtering it through the chemicals of her mind, would the warmth come back? Or would it just fade into the white of her unseeing eyes?

She waited, the blue light humming around them, the silence heavy and full of unsaid things.

---

The heavy silence shattered with a sharp *clatter*.

Evelyn looked down to see a wide-mouthed jar of cyanide solution tipping over the desk. The liquid, which she knew was just the printing fluid they'd used for that photo, spilled onto the wood, sizzling softly. But the sky outside the window was the one that made her heart stutter. The brilliant blue-and-white world she lived in was being overwritten; the azure was bleeding into a bruising purple-grey.

She watched in horror as the spilled fluid hit the floorboards. Instantly, the room didn't just change color; it froze. The chaotic motion of the falling jar, the sudden gasp of her companion, the way the light fractured on the spilled droplets—all of it locked into place with a crystalline clarity she'd never seen before. It was a permanent blueprint, cold and sharp, holding the moment in suspension.

Her companion stared at the mess, his face pale in the grey light, trying to laugh it off. But Evelyn saw the trap he'd set. By dropping the jar, he hadn't just spilled ink; he had triggered the chemical reaction that turned memory into stone. If they waited, the story would remain frozen in this perfect, cold blue-and-white sketch forever. The warmth of his laughter would fade into the white of her unseeing eyes, trapped in a frame that could never be touched.

She reached out, her fingers trembling, ready to catch the jar before it hit the floor. She had to let it fall. She had to let the story unfold. But as the grey light swallowed the room, she realized she was already too late. The splash had already happened, and now she had to watch it all turn to stone.

---

Evelyn watched the splash freeze mid-air, turning to grey stone the moment it touched the floor. The blue-and-white world felt suddenly brittle, like glass held too long in a freezer.

Then the laughter came. It wasn't the sharp, electric crackle of the cyanotype Evelyn usually saw. This was warmth, a low, rolling sound that vibrated against her chest. She smiled, her eyes closed, trying to match the feeling to the picture.

She reached for the jar of the fixative, her fingers trembling. If she poured it now, the warmth would harden into permanent blue. If she didn't, the memory would vanish. She had to let it fall.

With a soft cry, she tipped the jar. The liquid didn't dry; it spread. Slowly, beneath the stubborn blue, faint traces of amber and gold began to bleed through the ink. The "picture" wasn't failing; it was remembering. Evelyn realized then that the warmth wasn't in the chemicals at all. It was in her own memory, the very thing holding the color alive. Even in a world of cold light, they were the color that mattered. She looked at the spreading gold, a single, perfect ray of sun breaking through the grey, and knew she had finally caught it.
